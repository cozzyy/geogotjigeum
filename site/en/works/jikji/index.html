The Story

Finish reading Jikji: Amor Manet and you naturally start wondering exactly where the real history ends and the novel's imagination begins.

Jikji is the kind of name you learn as a single textbook line back in school — 'the world's oldest metal-type book, 78 years before Gutenberg' — and move on. Reading this novel and then digging into it, though, there turned out to be far more concrete, and honestly moving, facts behind that one line.

Cheongju's Heungdeoksa temple site isn't a setting the novel invented. A 1985 excavation turned up a bronze gong actually inscribed with the temple's name, finally confirming the location of a temple that had, until then, only existed in old records. The novel starting its story here wasn't arbitrary.

The physical Jikji volume still exists today — just not in Korea. It's held at the National Library of France. It was donated there in 1952 per the will of its former owner, Henri Vever, and sat in the stacks as just another old book for nearly two decades until a librarian named Dr. Park Byeong-seon first identified what it actually was, in 1972.

프랑스 국립도서관 리슐리외관 살 오발(직지가 실제 소장된 곳), 파리 · Wikimedia Commons

That part of the story sits with you for a moment. The fact that the world's oldest metal-type book isn't even in the country that made it, and that the person who recognized what it was happened to be a Korean researcher working as a librarian far from home. The novel builds a fictional present-day murder investigation on top of this real history.

The novel's central mystery — an old letter from the Vatican, and a direct link tying that letter to Gutenberg — is author Kim Jin-myung's invention. Neither the novel nor this site treats that as confirmed historical fact, and it's worth being clear about that.

That said, the invention isn't pulled entirely out of thin air. Gutenberg completed the 42-line Bible in his hometown of Mainz, but before that, real history shows he spent nearly a decade, starting in 1434, secretly experimenting with movable type in Strasbourg. The novel builds Eun-su — Karena's — European storyline on top of this confirmed record.

Whether there was ever a direct transmission between Jikji and Gutenberg's technology hasn't been confirmed by scholars. But the plain fact that two printing revolutions happened at opposite ends of Eurasia, 78 years apart, isn't fiction — that part is just history. Thinking about that distance and that gap in time, it's strange to imagine they're truly unconnected, and there's a certain pull to imagining they might somehow be linked after all.

The novel moves by interweaving the present-day murder investigation with the eras of King Sejong's Joseon and 15th-century Europe. The early stretch, where journalist Kim Ki-yeon — investigating the death of Professor Jeon Hyeong-woo — meets researcher Kim Jeong-jin at Cheongju's Heungdeoksa site and printing museum and edges closer to the truth about Jikji, is fiction, but it sits naturally on top of the real excavation history described above.

구텐베르크 박물관의 옛 인쇄기, 마인츠(독일) · CC BY 2.0 · Wikimedia Commons

From there, Kim Ki-yeon traces the whereabouts of the real Jikji volume at the National Library of France in Paris, while Eun-su's European storyline gets tangled up in Avignon and Strasbourg — a stretch that also happens to be a journey through real, existing places, one by one.

The section where Professor Fischer surfaces clues connecting Gutenberg and Mainz, and where the secret of the old Vatican letter starts coming to light through a character named Pemberton, is where the novel's mystery-genre machinery works hardest. I won't reveal here exactly what truth lies behind Jeon Hyeong-woo's death — that part is worth reading for yourself.

But what stays with you after closing the book isn't so much who the culprit was, as the sense of how a single book, made by unnamed monks at Heungdeoksa, ended up standing alongside the history of human printing itself, 78 years ahead of its time.

So if you trace this novel's route from Cheongju to Paris, Avignon, Strasbourg, and Mainz, sorting out what's confirmed history and what's the novel's imagination as you go will make the trip feel a lot deeper.

Standing at the Heungdeoksa site in Cheongju in particular, it hits you that this isn't just a novel's backdrop — it's the actual spot where the history of printing, for the whole world, began. That fact alone is enough to make the trip worthwhile.

FILMED HERE

흥덕사지 (충북 청주시 흥덕구 직지대로 713)

The site of the temple where the world's first metal-type printed book, Jikji, was actually published in 1377. A 1985 excavation confirmed the location after uncovering building foundations and a bronze gong inscribed with the temple's name, "Heungdeoksa." In the novel, this is where researcher Kim Jeong-jin explains Jikji's history to journalist Kim Ki-yeon — the story's real starting point. The boulevard running past the site is literally named "Jikji-daero," a sign of how central Jikji is to Cheongju's identity.

FILMED HERE

청주고인쇄박물관 (흥덕사지 옆)

A museum opened in 1992 right next to the Heungdeoksa site, presenting Jikji and Goryeo-era metal-type printing through artifacts, replicas, and digital immersive exhibits. In the novel, this is where Cheongju-based Jikji researcher Kim Jeong-jin is depicted working, complete with AR interactive displays. The most direct place to see where the world's oldest metal-type printing technology actually originated.

HANDS-ON

운리단길 (청주시 흥덕구 운천동)

A trendy alley district in Uncheon-dong near the Heungdeoksa site and the printing museum, where dozens of cafés, restaurants, and craft shops take part in a street festival during the Jikji Culture Festival. It doesn't appear directly in the novel, but it's a great way to experience everyday Cheongju life after exploring Jikji's history.

HANDS-ON

직지문화특구·직지문화축제 개최지 (청주 흥덕구 일대)

A cultural district designated by Cheongju around the Heungdeoksa site and printing museum, home to the annual Jikji Culture Festival held each autumn under the theme "Jikji, the Spark of Civilization." Programs include hands-on metal-type printing and a night exhibition at the temple's main hall site. Separate from the novel's plot, it's where visitors today can feel that Jikji is still a living civic festival, not just history.

HANDS-ON

청주 문화제조창 (청주시 청원구, 옛 연초제조창)

A former tobacco factory remodeled into a cultural complex, home to the Cheongju branch of the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art. If Jikji represents old printing technology, this is another example of Cheongju turning old industrial heritage into today's cultural content. It doesn't appear in the novel, but makes a great bonus stop for feeling both old and new Cheongju in one visit.

HANDS-ON

상당산성 (청주시 상당구)

A well-known mountain fortress from the Joseon dynasty, popular for its walking trail along the walls and views over Cheongju. If Jikji represents Cheongju's intellectual and cultural pride, Sangdangsanseong shows another side of the city's long defensive history. Not related to the novel directly, but a staple recommendation for anyone spending more than a day in Cheongju.

THEMED

고려대학교 (서울 성북구)

Depicted as the university where Latin professor Jeon Hyeong-woo taught while researching the connection between Jikji and Gutenberg's metal type, along with an old letter from the Vatican. His death here is the real starting point of the present-day storyline, from which journalist Kim Ki-yeon's investigation unfolds.

HANDS-ON

국립중앙박물관 (서울 용산구)

Home to a permanent exhibition on Goryeo and Joseon metal-type printing, where visitors can see the real technology behind Jikji's story. It isn't a confirmed scene from the novel, but it's the most reliable place in Seoul to see the subject Kim Ki-yeon and Kim Jeong-jin are chasing — Goryeo-era metal-type printing — in person.

HANDS-ON

주한프랑스대사관 (서울 서초구)

The fact that the original Jikji is held at the National Library of France is also tied to an ongoing conversation between Korea and France about cultural property repatriation. Not a confirmed scene from the novel, but this embassy is included as a real symbol of the kind of steps (visas, requests for cooperation) Ki-yeon would plausibly have gone through before heading to Paris.

FILMED HERE

Bibliothèque nationale de France, Site Richelieu

The real home of the surviving second volume of Jikji (catalogue number 109). Donated to the library in 1952 per the will of its owner Henri Vever, who died in 1943, it was here in 1972 that librarian Dr. Park Byeong-seon first identified Jikji and proved it predated Gutenberg's 42-line Bible by 78 years. One of the real destinations on Ki-yeon's European trail following Jeon Hyeong-woo's clues.

THEMED

Palais des Papes, Avignon

The site of the 14th-century "Avignon Papacy," when the Roman papacy relocated here. In the novel, it's woven into Eunsu (Karena)'s European storyline and the trail of clues surrounding an old Vatican letter. One of the largest Gothic palaces in Europe, still conveying the scale of medieval papal power today.

FILMED HERE

Strasbourg, France

Where Gutenberg is believed to have secretly begun his movable-type printing experiments from around 1434 to 1444 — he reportedly called the venture "Art and Enterprise" with his business partners. He later returned to his hometown of Mainz to complete the 42-line Bible. In the novel, this is where researcher Professor Fischer provides clues linking Karena to European printing.

FILMED HERE

Gutenberg-Museum, Mainz

One of the oldest printing museums in the world, holding two of the surviving 49 original copies of the 42-line Bible that Gutenberg completed in Mainz between 1452 and 1455 after returning home in 1448. Live printing demonstrations are held on reconstructed Gutenberg presses. The novel never claims Jikji's technology was directly transmitted to Gutenberg — but the fact that two printing revolutions happened 78 years apart at opposite ends of Eurasia makes this the essential counterpart stop on Jikji's journey.

HANDS-ON

Mainzer Dom, Mainz

A cathedral more than a thousand years old, right next to the Gutenberg Museum, where Gutenberg is believed to have been baptized. Not a specific scene from the novel, but a natural stop right next door if you're visiting the Gutenberg Museum.

THEMED

Vatican Apostolic Library and Archives

Where the novel's central mystery — an old papal letter — points to. In reality one of the world's oldest and most extensive document collections, holding many records of medieval contact between Europe and Asia, making it fertile ground for the novel's imagination. To be clear, though: the letter's recipient and interpretation in the story are entirely fictional, not a confirmed historical fact.
