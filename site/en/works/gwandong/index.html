I always thought Gwandong Byeolgok was just another classical poem we had to memorize in school — rhythm patterns, 3-4 syllable meter, all that.

Turns out, looking at it again years later, it's not just a poem. It's an actual travel record.

In 1580, 45-year-old Jeong Cheol was appointed governor of Gangwon Province, and this poem traces, almost step by step, the route he genuinely walked.

It opens well too. Jeong Cheol, moved by the king's summons, sets out for the provincial office in Wonju — so from the very first line, this poem never leaves the frame of 'a trip I actually took.'

The second section moves into Inner Kumgang. He climbs Manpokdong, Geumgangdae, Jinheoldae, and Gaesimdae in awe — and it struck me that every one of these is a real coordinate.

낙산사 경내 전경, 양양(관동팔경) · Wikimedia Commons

Same with Hwaryongyeon and the Twelve-Bend Waterfall. It's not just 'wow, a nice waterfall' — he names each specific spot as he goes.

From the third section on, he passes through Outer Kumgang and Haegeumgang and follows the East Coast through the Eight Views of Gwandong — and this is where it gets a little complicated.

Two of those eight, Chongseokjeong and Samilpo, can't be reached today no matter what map you pull up. They're in North Korea now.

Knowing that Chongseokjeong — where he marveled at the sunrise ringed by stone pillars rising out of the sea — now sits on the other side of a border completely changed how that passage read to me.

Same with Samilpo, tied to a legend of Silla-era warrior-monks. You simply can't go there anymore.

낙산사 원통보전, 양양(관동팔경) · Wikimedia Commons

The other six, though — Gyeongpodae, Naksansa, Jukseoru, Cheongganjeong, Mangyangjeong, Wolsongjeong — are all still there, exactly as he described.

The most famous passage of all is the one about Gyeongpodae — the moon in the sky, the moon reflected in the lake, the moon caught in a wine cup — the 'three moons.' What got me is that if you stand at Gyeongpodae today, you can still see this exact scene play out.

The sunrise from Uisangdae at Naksansa Temple, Jukseoru perched over the cliffs of the Osip Stream — all of it is unchanged. The scenery Jeong Cheol saw 440 years ago is the same scenery I could see today.

The poem's final section ends with him, having finished the real journey, dreaming of wandering with immortals. I think it's his way of saying the trip is over but the feeling isn't — and apparently there's a quiet undertone of political worry folded in there too.

And once I learned that Kim Man-jung praised this work in Seopo Manpil as 'the Li Sao of the East,' it stopped feeling like some old textbook poem and started feeling like a genuinely major work.

Realizing this one poem was a real 440-year-old travel itinerary completely changes how you look at a trip along the East Coast. Next time I'm in Gangneung, I want to stand at Gyeongpodae and actually look for those three moons.

강원감영 (강원 원주시 일산동)

The provincial government office in Wonju where the 45-year-old Jeong Cheol arrived after being appointed governor of Gangwon-do in the first month of 1580. Gwandong Byeolgok opens with the scene of him receiving the king's summons and setting out for this post.

RELATED

만폭동 (내금강, 북한)

Considered the finest valley in Inner Kumgang. In the poem's second section, Jeong Cheol marvels at this valley as he begins his tour of the Diamond Mountains.

RELATED

금강대 (내금강, 북한)

One of the peaks of Inner Kumgang, described in the poem as a place so sublime that immortals are said to ride cranes there.

RELATED

진헐대 (내금강, 북한)

One of the scenic overlooks of Inner Kumgang, where Jeong Cheol takes in the view of the Diamond Mountains' peaks and expresses his admiration.

RELATED

개심대 (내금강, 북한)

A scenic overlook whose name means 'the terrace that opens the mind' — where the poem describes gazing out over all twelve thousand peaks of the Diamond Mountains at once.

RELATED

화룡연 (내금강, 북한)

A pool linked to a legend of a dragon ascending to heaven — one of the mystical waterfall-and-pool scenes described in the poem.

RELATED

십이폭포 (내금강, 북한)

A waterfall said to cascade in twelve bends, described in the poem's second section as one of its most dramatic sights.

RELATED

총석정 (관동팔경, 강원 통천군, 북한)

One of the Eight Views of Gwandong — a pavilion surrounded by hexagonal stone pillars rising from the sea. Jeong Cheol describes watching the sunrise here.

RELATED

삼일포 (관동팔경, 강원 고성군, 북한)

A lake named for a legend that Silla-era hwarang warriors stayed here for three days. One of the Eight Views of Gwandong, painted in the poem as a mystical lakeside scene.

FILMED HERE

의상대 (양양 낙산사 경내)

A pavilion on a coastal cliff within Naksansa Temple, linked to the legend that the Silla monk Uisang meditated here. Famous as the spot where Jeong Cheol describes his awe at watching the sunrise over the East Sea.

RELATED

청간정 (관동팔경, 강원 고성군 간성)

One of the Eight Views of Gwandong — a pavilion where Jeong Cheol describes the meeting of a clear stream and the sea.

RELATED

낙산사 (관동팔경, 강원 양양)

A temple founded by the monk Uisang during the reign of Silla's King Munmu — one of the Eight Views of Gwandong. Jeong Cheol visits and describes its scenery in the poem.

RELATED

경포대 (관동팔경, 강원 강릉)

One of the Eight Views of Gwandong — a pavilion overlooking both Gyeongpo Lake and the East Sea. Jeong Cheol is famous for describing three moons here: one in the sky, one on the lake, and one on the sea.

RELATED

죽서루 (관동팔경, 강원 삼척)

A pavilion built atop a cliff over the Osip Stream, using the natural bedrock as part of its foundation — one of the Eight Views of Gwandong, praised in the poem for its striking setting.

RELATED

망양정 (관동팔경, 경북 울진)

One of the Eight Views of Gwandong, sometimes called the finest pavilion in the region. One of the last stops on the poem's journey, where Jeong Cheol sings of the vastness of the East Sea.

RELATED

월송정 (관동팔경, 경북 울진 평해)

A pavilion surrounded by a dense pine grove and white sand beach — one of the Eight Views of Gwandong, mentioned near the end of the poem's journey.
