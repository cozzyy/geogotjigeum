The places in the novel are still standing

Gwangju, May 1980. A novel that follows middle-schooler Dong-ho, who stays behind at Sangmugwan to help identify the dead after losing his friend Jeong-… Real filming spots, story settings and nearby travel ideas — clearly labeled.

After reading Human Acts, you naturally find yourself looking up where this story actually took place.

The novel isn't told by one person, but through six alternating voices: Jeong-dae, shot dead by martial law soldiers; Dong-ho, who dies helping identify the bodies at Sangmugwan; the survivors Eun-sook, Jin-su, and Seon-ju, each carrying that day for the rest of their lives; and finally, a mother who has lost her son.

Sangmugwan, where Dong-ho helps identify the dead in the novel, isn't an invented space. It still stands today, inside the restored Old Jeonnam Provincial Office complex, as Exhibition Hall 5 of the 5·18 Archives.

Dong-ho is widely known to be modeled on a real victim, Moon Jae-hak — sixteen years old at the time, a first-year student at Gwangju Commercial High School.

국립5·18민주묘지 추모탑, 광주 · Wikimedia Commons

The novel doesn't shy away from the fact that Dong-ho dies in the early hours of May 27, during the crackdown on the Provincial Office. His family begged him to leave. He stayed anyway, and that was the end for him.

Nor does it look away from what happens to the survivors. Eun-sook goes on to fight censorship at a publishing house, carrying the memory of that day with her. Jin-su survives detention and torture but is undone by guilt and takes his own life. Seon-ju devotes herself to human rights work but is never fully free of what the torture left behind.

The first time I read this novel, I didn't fully understand why it spends so much time on what happens to these people afterward. Reading it again, I understood: the story it's telling isn't only 'what happened that day,' but 'how people went on living for decades after.'

The novel alludes several times to helicopter gunfire — a claim long denied. Looking into it, I learned that physical evidence for it still exists. A 2016-2017 forensic investigation confirmed 245 bullet marks on the Jeonil Building; that's where the building's current name, 'Jeonil Building 245,' comes from.

I also learned that the National 5·18 Cemetery and the old Mangwol-dong cemetery are two different places. When the new national cemetery was built in 1997, most of the remains were relocated there, but the original burial ground still exists separately.

국립5·18민주묘지 묘역, 광주 · Wikimedia Commons

Since 2025, the city of Gwangju has divided the novel's settings into two official literary walking routes: the 'Boy's Path' (5·18 historic sites) and the 'Author's Path' (the neighborhood where Han Kang actually grew up).

Knowing that the author herself had already moved to Seoul before the uprising happened — and wasn't there — makes you reconsider why she carried this story regardless, and what it means to hold onto something you didn't personally experience, this precisely and for this long.

After Han Kang won the Nobel Prize in 2024, Moon Jae-hak's mother speaking to the press about her son and this novel drew renewed attention around the same time.

This site doesn't present this journey as a pilgrimage or a photo tour. It's a walk through ground where a massacre and an uprising actually happened.

Reading the novel and walking through Gwangju is closer to facing, together, what happened there, the long silence that followed, and the record that remains today. What Dong-ho's mother never manages to let go of, even after the last page — that's something only a reader of the novel itself can really know.

상무관 (5·18민주화운동기록관 옛 전남도청 별관, 광주 동구 문화전당로 38)

From May 22, 1980, citizens voluntarily gathered and identified the dead here, laying them in coffins at a makeshift memorial altar. This is the exact space where the novel's Dong-ho, Eun-sook, and Jin-su help wash and identify bodies — the emotional center of the entire novel. Today it forms part of the restored Old Jeonnam Provincial Office complex (Exhibition Hall 5 of the 5·18 Archives).

RELATED

옛 전남도청 (국립아시아문화전당 내, 광주 동구 문화전당로 38)

Where martial law troops crushed the last of the citizen militia in the early morning of May 27, 1980. In the novel, this is where Dong-ho refuses to leave and is ultimately killed. Real victims — including 16-year-old Moon Jae-hak — died here; the building was restored and reopened in the 2020s as a 5·18 exhibition space.

RELATED

5·18민주광장 (광주 동구, 옛 전남도청 앞)

The square where mass citizen rallies took place in May 1980, and where the official annual commemoration is still held every May 18. The real backdrop for the novel's scenes of citizens gathering in front of the provincial office to confront martial law troops.

RELATED

금남로 (광주 동구, 옛 전남도청~광주역 방면)

The central street of downtown Gwangju and the site of martial law troops' mass shooting on May 21, 1980. This is also the street where the novel's Jeong-dae is shot dead. Forensic investigation later confirmed that soldiers fired on citizens from the rooftops of buildings lining the street, including the Jeonil Building and the old Suhyup building.

RELATED

전일빌딩245 (광주 동구 금남로 245)

A 2016-2017 National Forensic Service investigation confirmed 245 bullet marks on this building, which gave it its current name. The 10th floor preserves the physical evidence of helicopter gunfire as the "5·18 Memory Space" — key physical proof supporting long-denied claims that martial law helicopters fired on the building.

RELATED

5·18민주화운동기록관 (광주 동구 금남로 221)

The official archive, housed in the former Gwangju Catholic Center building. Its holdings were inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2011. Much of the testimony and material Han Kang drew on while writing the novel traces back to records held here.

RELATED

전남대학교 정문 (광주 북구 용봉로 77)

Where martial law troops clubbed students protesting the campus closure on the morning of May 18, 1980. The wounded students scattered into Geumnam-ro, and this moment became the spark that turned the crackdown into a citywide uprising.

RELATED

옛 광주적십자병원 (광주 동구 제봉로 42)

One of the main hospitals that treated wounded citizens in May 1980. As of 2026, it is being redeveloped into the "5·18 Daedong Peace Hall," preserving the original operating room and ICU.

RELATED

광주극장 (광주 동구 충장로46번길 10)

Korea's oldest single-screen theater, opened in 1935 and still operating. Not a direct 1980 incident site, but every May it runs "May Cinema," screening 5·18-related films and documentaries — a living hub of ongoing memorial culture, including past reading events tied to Human Acts.

RELATED

국립5·18민주묘지 (광주 북구 민주로 200)

A national cemetery opened in 1997, to which 125 sets of remains were relocated from the original Mangwol-dong burial ground. Real victims, including Moon Jae-hak, are interred here. Note: this is a different location from the "old Mangwol-dong cemetery" below — a common point of public confusion.

RELATED

5·18구묘역 (옛 망월동 묘지, 광주 북구 망월동 산 176-1)

The original burial ground where victims moved from Sangmugwan were first interred on May 29, 1980. Most remains were relocated when the new national cemetery was built in 1997, but this site retains its own historical significance as the real ground of the 1980s-90s truth-seeking movement.

RELATED

광주공원 (광주 남구 서서평길 7)

After the mass shooting of May 21, citizens organized into a militia here, arming themselves from nearby reserve-forces armories and conducting shooting drills. It served as a base for self-defense until the militia consolidated at the provincial office on May 24.

RELATED

광주기독병원 (광주 남구 양림로 116)

Founded in 1905 by American Southern Presbyterian missionaries, this hospital treated wounded citizens and militia members during the uprising. Designated 5·18 Historic Site No. 10. Located within the Yangnim-dong missionary quarter.

RELATED

조선대학교 (광주 동구 필문대로 309)

Along with Chonnam National University, a campus occupied by martial law troops (7th Airborne Brigade) from the evening of May 17, 1980 — also a hub of the pre-uprising campus democratization movement.

RELATED

한강 유년기 거주지·효동초등학교 (광주 북구 중흥동 일대)

The neighborhood where author Han Kang lived and the elementary school she attended before her family moved to Seoul in 1979. A core stretch of the city's official "Author's Path" — a place that shows why a writer who wasn't in Gwangju during the uprising still carried this story for life. Her original home is now a different shop.
